## ABSTRACT
Background: Basaloid squamous cell carcinoma (BSCC) of the uterine cervix is a rare variant of squamous cell carcinoma characterized by nests of small basaloid cells that can closely mimic small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (SCNEC) morphologically. Accurate distinction between these entities is critical, as they require fundamentally different treatment approaches.
## Case Presentation: 
- We report a case of a 40-year-old woman presenting with cervical enlargement and erosion. Histopathologic examination revealed small cell tumor components with stromal invasion at multiple cervical sites (3, 6, 8, and 12 o'clock positions), accompanied by high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL/CIN3). The tumor demonstrated scattered positivity for INSM1 and CD56, raising diagnostic concern for SCNEC. However, comprehensive immunohistochemical analysis revealed negativity for synaptophysin, chromogranin A, and TTF-1, with positive expression of P40, CK5/6, and broad-spectrum cytokeratin. The tumor also showed diffuse p16 positivity, a Ki-67 proliferation index of approximately 80%, and p53 expression in approximately 50% of tumor cells.
- Diagnosis and Discussion: Based on the integrated morphologic and immunohistochemical profile, particularly the absence of classic neuroendocrine markers (synaptophysin, chromogranin A, TTF-1) and the presence of squamous markers (P40, CK5/6), a diagnosis of basaloid squamous cell carcinoma was rendered. The scattered INSM1 and CD56 positivity was interpreted as aberrant focal neuroendocrine marker expression, a well-documented phenomenon in BSCC. The coexistence of HSIL/CIN3 further supported squamous lineage. This case highlights critical diagnostic pitfalls in distinguishing BSCC from SCNEC and emphasizes the importance of comprehensive immunohistochemical panels and correlation with morphologic context.
## Conclusion: 
- BSCC of the cervix can exhibit focal neuroendocrine marker expression and small cell carcinoma-like morphologic features, particularly in biopsy specimens. Recognition of this diagnostic pitfall is essential to avoid misclassification as SCNEC, which would lead to inappropriate neuroendocrine-specific chemotherapy. A systematic approach integrating morphology, comprehensive immunohistochemistry, and clinical context is required for accurate diagnosis.
Keywords: Basaloid squamous cell carcinoma; Small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma; Uterine cervix; INSM1; CD56; Immunohistochemistry; Differential diagnosis

## INTRODUCTION
- Cervical cancer remains the fourth most common malignancy among women globally, with squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) accounting for approximately 80-90% of cases [1]. Within the spectrum of cervical SCC, basaloid squamous cell carcinoma (BSCC) represents a rare and morphologically distinctive variant characterized by nests of small basaloid cells with high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratios, peripheral palisading, and minimal stromal reaction [2,3]. The majority of cervical SCCs, including BSCC, are associated with high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) infection, particularly HPV types 16 and 18, and demonstrate diffuse p16 overexpression by immunohistochemistry [1,4].
- Small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (SCNEC) of the cervix, although rare (comprising 5% of cervical cancers), represents the most common site for neuroendocrine carcinoma in the female genital tract [1]. SCNEC is a highly aggressive malignancy with rapid metastatic potential and poor prognosis, requiring neuroendocrine-specific multimodal therapy with platinum/etoposide-based chemotherapy [5,6]. The morphologic overlap between BSCC and SCNEC, particularly in small biopsy specimens, creates a significant diagnostic challenge with profound therapeutic implications.
- The differential diagnosis between BSCC and SCNEC is further complicated by the phenomenon of aberrant neuroendocrine marker expression in BSCC. Recent studies have demonstrated that basaloid squamous cell carcinomas can exhibit focal positivity for CD56 and even INSM1, markers traditionally considered specific for neuroendocrine differentiation [7]. Ishida et al. reported that small cell carcinoma-like features were identified in 80% of esophageal BSCC biopsies, with CD56 expression in 42% of cases, while synaptophysin and chromogranin A remained consistently negative [7]. This diagnostic pitfall is particularly relevant in the era of expanded neuroendocrine marker panels, as scattered INSM1 positivity may be misinterpreted as evidence of neuroendocrine differentiation.
- Herein, we report a challenging case of cervical BSCC in a 40-year-old woman that demonstrated scattered INSM1 and CD56 positivity, initially raising concern for SCNEC. Through comprehensive immunohistochemical analysis and correlation with morphologic features, we established the correct diagnosis of BSCC. This case illustrates critical diagnostic pitfalls and provides a systematic approach to distinguishing these morphologically similar but therapeutically distinct entities.

## CASE PRESENTATION
### Clinical History
- A 40-year-old woman presented for gynecologic evaluation. Physical examination revealed cervical enlargement and erosion. No other specific symptoms were reported, and imaging studies were not available at the time of initial pathologic evaluation. The patient underwent cervical biopsy at four quadrants (3, 6, 8, and 12 o'clock positions) for histopathologic examination.
### Histopathologic Findings
Microscopic examination of the cervical biopsies revealed the following findings:
- 3 o'clock position: The squamous epithelium showed a prominent small cell tumor component with stromal invasion. The tumor was composed of nests and cords of small basaloid cells with scant cytoplasm, hyperchromatic nuclei, and fine granular chromatin. Peripheral palisading of tumor cells was noted at the edges of some tumor nests. Stromal invasion was highly suspicious. Adjacent areas demonstrated high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL/CIN3) with full-thickness dysplasia and loss of normal squamous maturation.
- 6 o'clock position: HSIL/CIN3 was identified, characterized by full-thickness atypia of the squamous epithelium with increased nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratios, nuclear hyperchromasia, and numerous mitotic figures extending to the superficial layers.
8 o'clock position: Focal intraepithelial squamous carcinoma with migratory growth pattern was observed. The malignant squamous cells were confined within the epithelium but showed infiltrative growth along the basement membrane zone, a pattern sometimes referred to as "squamous carcinoma growing within the epithelium."
- 12 o'clock position: Free-floating nests of prominent small cell tumor component with stromal invasion were identified. The morphology was similar to that observed at the 3 o'clock position. Adjacent HSIL/CIN3 was also present.
- The most significant pathologic finding was the small cell tumor component with stromal invasion identified at the 3 and 12 o'clock positions. The morphologic differential diagnosis included small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (SCNEC) versus basaloid squamous cell carcinoma (BSCC). The coexistence of HSIL/CIN3 at multiple sites suggested a squamous lineage; however, definitive classification required comprehensive immunohistochemical analysis.
### Immunohistochemical Findings
A comprehensive immunohistochemical panel was performed to distinguish between SCNEC and BSCC.
#### Neuroendocrine Markers:
•	Synaptophysin (Syn): Negative
•	Chromogranin A (CgA): Negative
•	CD56: Positive (scattered tumor cells showing membranous staining)
•	INSM1: Positive (scattered tumor cells showing nuclear staining)
•	TTF-1: Negative
#### Squamous Markers:
•	P40: Positive (scattered tumor cells showing nuclear staining)
•	CK5/6: Positive (diffuse cytoplasmic staining)
•	Broad-spectrum cytokeratin (CK): Positive (diffuse cytoplasmic staining)
#### Additional Markers:
•	p16: Positive (diffuse strong nuclear and cytoplasmic staining)
•	p53: Positive (nuclear staining in approximately 50% of tumor cells)
•	Ki-67: Approximately 80% proliferation index
### Final Diagnosis
Based on the integrated morphologic and immunohistochemical findings, a final diagnosis of basaloid squamous cell carcinoma (BSCC) of the uterine cervix with stromal invasion was rendered. The tumor was accompanied by high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL/CIN3) at multiple cervical sites and focal intraepithelial squamous carcinoma with migratory growth.
The diagnosis of small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma was excluded based on the following key features:
1	Negativity for classic neuroendocrine markers (synaptophysin, chromogranin A, TTF-1)
2	Positive expression of squamous markers (P40, CK5/6, broad-spectrum cytokeratin)
3	Coexistence with HSIL/CIN3, a squamous precursor lesion
4	Scattered rather than diffuse INSM1 and CD56 positivity

### DISCUSSION
#### Basaloid Squamous Cell Carcinoma: A Rare Variant With Diagnostic Challenges
- Basaloid squamous cell carcinoma (BSCC) of the uterine cervix is a rare and underrecognized variant of squamous cell carcinoma that poses significant diagnostic challenges, particularly in distinguishing it from small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (SCNEC) [1]. First described by Daroca and Dhurandhar in 1980, BSCC is characterized by nests or cords of small basaloid cells with prominent peripheral palisading and minimal stromal reaction [2]. The tumor demonstrates an ulcerated infiltrating growth pattern and is composed of cells with high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratios, hyperchromatic nuclei, and inconspicuous nucleoli—features that closely resemble SCNEC morphologically [2,3].
The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines recognize basaloid pattern as one of the histologic variants of cervical squamous cell carcinoma, noting that non-keratinizing and basaloid patterns are most commonly associated with HPV-associated tumors [1]. The 2020 World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Female Genital Tumors separates cervical squamous cell carcinomas by HPV status into HPV-associated (HPVA) and HPV-independent (HPVI) tumors, although morphology alone cannot reliably distinguish these categories [4,8]. Almost all HPV-associated SCCs, including BSCC, show strong and diffuse p16 overexpression by immunohistochemistry, reflecting the presence of high-risk HPV oncoproteins E6 and E7 [1,9].
- The present case exemplifies the diagnostic complexity of BSCC. The tumor demonstrated small cell morphology with stromal invasion at multiple cervical sites, accompanied by HSIL/CIN3—a squamous precursor lesion that is characteristic of HPV-associated squamous carcinogenesis and rarely seen in association with neuroendocrine carcinomas [4,10]. The diffuse p16 positivity in this case is consistent with HPV-associated BSCC, as reported in multiple studies demonstrating that BSCC is etiologically related to high-risk HPV infection [8,11].
#### Molecular Pathogenesis and HPV Association
- The molecular pathogenesis of BSCC is intimately linked to high-risk HPV infection. Shi et al. performed a comprehensive clinicopathological and molecular analysis of cervical carcinomas with basaloid features and found that high-risk HPV E6/E7 mRNA was detected in the majority of basaloid SCCs, with higher expression levels in the SCC components compared to adenoid basal-like components [4]. The study demonstrated that all components of mixed carcinomas consistently showed diffuse p16, p63, and SOX2 expression, supporting a common squamous lineage [4]. Missaoui et al. confirmed that HPV types 16/18 were detected in basaloid squamous cell carcinomas of the cervix, with strong and diffuse p16 overexpression in all tumor cells, similar to conventional cervical SCCs [8].
- The molecular mechanisms underlying HPV-associated cervical carcinogenesis involve the integration of HPV DNA into the host genome, resulting in overexpression of the E6 and E7 oncoproteins [9,10]. E6 interacts with and degrades p53, while E7 binds to and inactivates the retinoblastoma (Rb) protein, leading to deregulation of cell cycle control and genomic instability [9,10]. This results in overexpression of p16INK4a, a cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor, which serves as a surrogate marker for HPV-associated tumors [9,11]. The diffuse p16 positivity observed in this case is consistent with this molecular mechanism and supports the diagnosis of HPV-associated BSCC.
Morphologic Overlap and the Small Cell Carcinoma-Like Phenotype
- One of the most critical diagnostic pitfalls in this case was the presence of small cell carcinoma-like morphologic features in the BSCC. Ishida et al. demonstrated that small cell carcinoma-like features—including diffuse growth, nuclear molding, and crush artifact—were identified in 80% of esophageal BSCC biopsies and in 77% of surgical specimens, but in a proportionally much smaller area in surgical specimens than in biopsy samples [7]. The authors concluded that these features are often attributable to exogenous factors such as friction and external pressure that occur during the biopsy procedure and in the tumor microenvironment, and do not impact prognosis [7].
- Grayson and Cooper provided a comprehensive review of basaloid cervical neoplasms and emphasized that BSCC represents the aggressive end of the spectrum of basaloid tumors, distinct from low-grade lesions such as adenoid basal carcinoma [3]. They recommended use of the term "basaloid squamous cell carcinoma" to avoid confusion and proposed a classification system for basaloid tumors of the cervix [3]. The key distinguishing features include the presence of squamous differentiation, association with HSIL/CIN3, and expression of squamous markers.
#### Immunohistochemical Differentiation: The Critical Role of Marker Panels
- The immunohistochemical profile in this case was decisive in establishing the diagnosis of BSCC and excluding SCNEC. The tumor demonstrated negativity for the classic neuroendocrine markers synaptophysin, chromogranin A, and TTF-1, while showing positive expression of squamous markers P40, CK5/6, and broad-spectrum cytokeratin. This immunoprofile is characteristic of BSCC and inconsistent with SCNEC.
- Neuroendocrine Markers in SCNEC: According to the NCCN guidelines, CD56 and synaptophysin are the most sensitive neuroendocrine markers for small cell NECC, with pooled positive rates of approximately 85% each, while chromogranin A is the most specific marker but is positive in only approximately 50-60% of cases [1]. A meta-analysis by Huang et al. confirmed that the simultaneous expression of synaptophysin and CD56 is observed in nearly 88% of SCNEC cases [16].
- INSM1: Sensitivity, Specificity, and Diagnostic Pitfalls: INSM1 (insulinoma-associated protein 1) is a transcription factor for neuroendocrine differentiation that has emerged as a promising diagnostic marker for neuroendocrine carcinomas [18,19,20]. However, the specificity of INSM1 is not absolute. Zou et al. specifically studied INSM1 in gynecologic high-grade neuroendocrine carcinomas and found that INSM1 had 92% sensitivity and 95% specificity, but emphasized that the mean percentage of INSM1-positive tumor cells in true SCNEC is approximately 54-60%, with diffuse and strong nuclear staining being characteristic [20]. Critically, the authors cautioned that focal or weak INSM1 staining limited to scattered cells is insufficient to establish neuroendocrine differentiation and can occur in non-neuroendocrine tumors [20]. This finding is directly applicable to the present case, where scattered INSM1 positivity was observed but did not meet the threshold for SCNEC diagnosis.
- Tsai et al. studied INSM1 expression in thoracic malignancies and found that weak, focal INSM1 expression alone is insufficient as a diagnostic marker for small cell carcinoma. Among non-neuroendocrine tumors, 12% of lung squamous cell carcinomas showed INSM1 expression [24].
- Squamous Markers: P40, CK5/6, and High Molecular Weight Cytokeratins: The expression of P40, CK5/6, and broad-spectrum cytokeratin in this case strongly supports squamous differentiation. P40 is a highly specific marker for squamous lineage and is characteristically negative in SCNEC, except in rare cases of aberrant expression [25]. Inzani et al. found that p63 and p40 expression were negative in all cervical neuroendocrine carcinomas studied [25]. Morice and Ferreiro demonstrated that the high-molecular-weight cytokeratin antibody 34βE12 was reactive in 22 of 23 cases of BSCC but in none of 10 cases of small cell undifferentiated carcinoma, establishing that cytokeratin expression patterns reliably differentiate these entities [26].
#### Treatment Implications: BSCC Versus SCNEC
- The distinction between BSCC and SCNEC has profound therapeutic implications. BSCC is treated according to standard squamous cell carcinoma protocols, while SCNEC requires neuroendocrine-specific multimodal therapy with platinum/etoposide-based chemotherapy [1,5,6].
- According to the NCCN guidelines, cervical squamous cell carcinoma (including BSCC as a variant) is treated based on FIGO stage [1]. For locally advanced disease, primary chemoradiation consisting of weekly cisplatin with external beam radiation therapy followed by brachytherapy is the standard of care.
- In contrast, SCNEC requires neuroendocrine-specific chemotherapy with etoposide 100 mg/m² IV on days 1-3 and cisplatin 75 mg/m² IV on day 1, repeated every 21 days for 4-6 cycles, in addition to surgery and/or radiation [1,32,33]. Chu et al. analyzed the SEER database and confirmed that chemotherapy was a protective factor across all stages of cervical small cell carcinoma [5]. The aggressive nature of SCNEC and its propensity for early distant metastasis necessitate neuroendocrine-specific systemic therapy, making accurate diagnosis critical.
#### Diagnostic Algorithm and Recommendations
- Based on the literature review and the present case, we propose the following diagnostic algorithm for distinguishing BSCC from SCNEC in cervical small cell tumors:
#### Morphologic Evaluation:
1	Assess growth pattern: BSCC typically shows nests or cords with peripheral palisading and minimal stromal reaction, while SCNEC shows predominantly diffuse growth.
2	Evaluate cytologic features: BSCC may show small cell carcinoma-like features in biopsies, but these are often exogenous artifacts.
3	Assess for associated lesions: The presence of HSIL/CIN3 strongly favors squamous lineage (BSCC).
#### Immunohistochemical Panel:
1	First-line neuroendocrine markers: Synaptophysin, chromogranin A, and INSM1. Diffuse positivity supports SCNEC; negativity argues against SCNEC.
2	Squamous markers: P40, CK5/6, and high molecular weight cytokeratins (34βE12). Diffuse positivity supports BSCC.
3	Additional markers: CD56 (sensitive but not specific), p16 (positive in both BSCC and SCNEC due to HPV association).
#### Interpretation Guidelines: 
- Scattered or focal INSM1 and/or CD56 positivity in the absence of synaptophysin and chromogranin A positivity is insufficient for SCNEC diagnosis and may represent aberrant expression in BSCC.

### CONCLUSION
This case illustrates the critical diagnostic challenge of distinguishing basaloid squamous cell carcinoma from small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma of the uterine cervix. The morphologic overlap between these entities, particularly in small biopsy specimens, can lead to diagnostic confusion. However, a systematic approach integrating morphology, comprehensive immunohistochemistry, and clinical context enables accurate diagnosis.
The key diagnostic features favoring BSCC in this case included: (1) coexistence with HSIL/CIN3, a squamous precursor lesion; (2) negativity for classic neuroendocrine markers synaptophysin, chromogranin A, and TTF-1; (3) positive expression of squamous markers P40, CK5/6, and broad-spectrum cytokeratin; and (4) scattered rather than diffuse INSM1 and CD56 positivity. Recognition of this diagnostic pitfall is essential to avoid misclassification as SCNEC, which would lead to inappropriate neuroendocrine-specific chemotherapy.
